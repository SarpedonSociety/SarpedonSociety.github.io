*Silversides* left Brisbane for her fourth patrol with a fresh refit, no training period, and a captain and a crew who had already been through three patrols together. The first week brought a shipboard appendectomy, a bombing that nearly wrenched the conning tower loose, and a mistaken attack on what proved to be a destroyer. Then came two weeks of nearly empty water off Truk. The patrol turned in its last ten days: a tanker blown up on 18 January, a convoy hit at dusk on 20 January, and then a string of failures caused by depth charging that forced her to leave the area early. Fifteen torpedoes went out and nine hit. COMSUBPAC called this "an excellent hit percentage of 60%."

## What the histories say

The *Dictionary of American Naval Fighting Ships* gives the patrol three paragraphs:

> Silversides departed Brisbane on 17 December and set course for New Ireland for her fourth war patrol. While far put at sea on the night of Christmas Eve, the submarine's pharmacist's mate performed a successful emergency appendectomy on one of the crewmen. With the operation over at 0400 on the 25th, the submarine surfaced only to be immediately forced down by a Japanese destroyer and compelled to endure a severe depth charge attack. Thinking herself safe, Silversides surfaced only to find the destroyer still there. In addition, a Japanese airplane had arrived on the scene, and proceeded to drop three bombs on the submarine, severely damaging her bow planes and causing them to lock on full dive. Silversides managed to level off just short of crush depth and eventually evaded the enemy ship before surfacing to recharge her batteries and effect emergency repairs.
>
> While off Truk on 18 January 1943, Silversides torpedoed and sank tanker Toei Maru. Two days later, the submarine had one of her most productive days of the war. After paralleling a convoy throughout the daylight hours, she moved on ahead at sundown to lay in wait. As the targets moved into range, she fired her torpedoes at overlapping targets and sank three enemy ships: the cargo ships Surabaya Maru, Somedono Maru, and Meiu Maru. The attack had scarcely abated when it was discovered that an armed torpedo was stuck in a forward torpedo tube. Since it was impossible to disarm the torpedo, the commanding officer decided to attempt to refire it, an extremely dangerous maneuver. The submarine moved in reverse at top speed and fired. The torpedo shot safely from the tube, disappearing as it moved toward the horizon.
>
> When a serious oil leak was discovered later that night, the submarine left the patrol area two days ahead of schedule and returned to Pearl Harbor on 31 January.

The patrol report corrects and expands this in several places:

- **The appendectomy and the destroyer were 22–23 December, not Christmas Eve.** The report puts the decision to operate at 2100 on 22 December and the dive at 2152. The destroyer episode came at 0357 on 23 December, about an hour after the operation ended. DANFS has the operation ending at 0400 on 25 December. DANFS also folds the destroyer, the depth charging and the bombing into one sequence. In the report *Silversides* first fired two torpedoes at the destroyer, taking it for a submarine, and the bombing came at 0856 during a submerged periscope sweep, not after surfacing.
- **Three ships "sunk" on 20 January.** DANFS names *Surabaya Maru*, *Somedono Maru* and *Meiu Maru*. COMSUBPAC at the time credited two cargo ships as damaged (7,210 and 5,155 tons) and called it probable that they sank.
- **The refired torpedo and the leaks were spread over two days.** The report finds the torpedo stuck half out of No. 6 tube on the evening of 21 January and fires it by backing emergency, and the fuel and air leaks surface that same day. The decision to leave came on 21–22 January and she left the area on 23 January.
- **The early departure was a bundle of failures, not one leak.** Fuel ran short because of four-engine chases; there were also air and fuel leaks, a generator explosion, a loose terminal on the main excitation switch, and a torpedo stuck half out of its tube. The commanding officer's governing-factor entry blames fuel shortage, leaks and electrical failures "apparently due to depth charging."
- **The credit was cautious.** COMSUBPAC credited one tanker sunk and everything else as damaged, because of "lack of sufficient observation." Postwar assessment (below) credits more.
- **The two targets that mattered most were both hit at long odds.** The tanker on 18 January was hit from a submerged approach with a destroyer escort astern; the convoy on 20 January was hit at night from the wrong side of the convoy's own geometry.

## Prologue: refit and departure

*Silversides* arrived at Brisbane on 25 November 1942 from her third patrol. USS *Sperry* began the refit that day and completed it on 9 December. She was ready for sea on 16 December. Departure slipped one day because of defects in the general announcing system that did not show up until departure day. She was not degaussed ("depermed nor wiped") and had no training period.

## Passage and the appendectomy (17–22 December)

She left Brisbane at 1400 on 17 December. En route she ran a test-depth dive, training dives and drills. On 20 December she sighted a U.S. task force of escorted battleships at 0730. The destroyers' recognition signals were not exchanged until the escorts had been in sight for half an hour, which Burlingame noted dryly. She ran two battle-surface drills by day and one at night and compensated her magnetic compass. On 21 December she began her submerged run off the Louisiades and sighted a low-wing monoplane at about 1016.

On the forenoon of 22 December, G.M. Platter was suspected of appendicitis. By late afternoon the attack was acute. At 2100 the decision was made to operate, and at 2152 *Silversides* submerged to give the surgeon a steadier platform. The operation was performed by Pharmacist's Mate First Class T. A. Moore on the wardroom table, at 100 feet in the channel off Rabaul so that the boat would not be surprised on the surface. Part XIII of the report gives the details:

- Spinal anesthetic was used first.
- The appendix had adhered to the intestines. Getting it clear, "part of the intestines popped out the cut."
- When the local anesthetic wore off, Moore gave ether "following directions on the can." The captain's comment: "This anesthetized the operating staff as well as the patient."
- The operation took four hours.
- One hour after it ended, *Silversides* was in a fight with a destroyer. The patient spent the next morning in his bunk through "a torpedo firing, two depth charge attacks, two crash dives and an aerial bombing which knocked him out of his bunk on the wardroom transom."

He was back on duty six days later. Burlingame wrote that the patient's conduct was "exemplary." COMSUBPAC's squadron endorsement commended Moore but added that such operations by other than qualified medical officers "are not encouraged."

## Attack 1 — a destroyer mistaken for a submarine (23 December)

At 0150 on 23 December *Silversides* surfaced and, at 0305, sighted a darkened ship ahead. All hands topside agreed it was a submarine. Because she was close to the western boundary of one area, Burlingame thought it probably friendly and decided to work around it to the west. At 0325, when she crossed the moon slick, the stranger sighted her and turned toward her. By 0340 he was overhauling her slowly even at 20 knots. Burlingame tried recognition signals "in chance he was friendly since we were definitely sighted any way." The reply was a green light flashing MO followed by characters not in the Morse Code.

At 0357 *Silversides* fired two torpedoes from the stern tubes at slow speed, gyro 180°, track angle zero. The first exploded prematurely about 2,000 yards astern, having porpoised in the early part of its run. The second was not observed. As the target closed, it became clear that he was not a submarine. *Silversides* dove to avoid depth charges. Three minutes later the first barrage of four charges went off astern. The target then passed directly over her and dropped another barrage ahead. There was no damage. The contact table lists him as a destroyer of the *Shinonome* type.

At 0507 the boat started up to periscope depth, and the noise of blowing and venting safety brought the destroyer back for another run, though he did not drop. Burlingame got to periscope depth at 0600 and sighted a two-stack destroyer at 0630, but its minimum range was 3,900 yards as it covered the area to the south. He broke off.

### The bombing (23 December, 0856)

At 0856, just after a periscope sweep, a plane dropped three bombs directly on top of her in rapid succession. Burlingame wrote: "I thought the conning tower was being wrenched loose from the pressure hull." Every light in the forward torpedo room and conning tower went out, men were thrown from their bunks and cut by flying glass, and both barometers were wrecked. The bow planes jammed on hard dive. The diving officer, Lt. R. K. R. Worthington, "did a remarkable job in getting to deep submergence in record time and holding the depth." After about 15 minutes the bow planes were brought back under hand control. The destroyer then came over and made two runs, dropping four or five charges, and pulled away to the northwest.

When *Silversides* surfaced at 1942 the full damage showed: the vent gasket on No. 1 main ballast tank blown out, the bridge loudspeakers on the 1MC wrecked, the starboard antenna carried away, the shear pins in the bow-plane rigging shaft in the superstructure sheared, and a cracked prism in No. 2 periscope. At 2033 radar picked up another plane and she made a quick dive at four miles. She made landfall on the Feni Islands at 2325. Part VIII lists the full bomb damage (see *Damage list*, below) and calls it "by far the closest any have come to date."

## Kavieng and the *Takasago Maru* chase (24–27 December)

She entered her area at 0100 on 24 December between Feni and New Ireland and ran up the north coast, repairing the bow planes that evening. At dawn on 25 December she was off Kavieng and began submerged patrol at 0530 when flashing lights were seen over the airfield. During the day she sighted two patrol planes, another taking off, a bomber landing, and smoke in Steffen Strait, but no shipping.

At 1818 she sighted a large two-stack passenger ship close to the coast of New Hanover, seven miles off and bearing 90° starboard. Later identified as the *Takasago Maru*, she was "listed as a hospital ship but range too great to see any funnel markings or painting." Burlingame could not get into position submerged, so he paralleled her, surfaced at 1948 and set out on four engines, estimating 15 knots on a course for Truk. He wrote that the chase was made "in the chance that he was no longer a hospital ship or that the circuit to his distinguishing lights was out of commission." She lost the ship overnight.

On 26 December, after reducing speed, she sighted smoke at 1024 and spent the rest of the day on the surface working around a one-stack freighter. Just at sunset the steamer disappeared into a rain squall. The squall became general heavy rain and *Silversides* held the target's base course until midnight, reaching the southern boundary of her area without seeing him again. Burlingame's complaint: after a day-long chase in excellent visibility it was hard to lose him in the rain, "especially since the SJ might have picked him up if the SJ would only work."

She could not enter the area until 28 December, so she patrolled an east–west line covering the Truk–Solomons route and sighted nothing.

## Off Truk (27 December – 16 January)

From 27 December she took station off Truk. Her main patrol line ran four to five miles west of Piannu Pass by day and retired west at night. For four days (28–31 December) there was no shipping, patrol vessels, planes or depth charges, only a searchlight on Tol one evening about 2000.

The weeks that followed were quiet and tense:

- **1 January.** At 0707 she sighted a tanker with a small patrol boat trailing it, heading for Aulap or Otta Pass. Angle on the bow was 120° port with the range 7,200 and opening. No attack was possible.
- **2 January.** Distant depth charging all day. To Burlingame it "sounded more like systematic barrages than random charges." A four-engine bomber went south at 0822.
- **4 January.** A patrol boat at 1009 close to Royalist Reef, then at 1128 a large ship coming out of Otta Pass that was identified as a hospital ship, "properly marked" (the *Yasina Maru*, per the contact table). *Silversides* photographed her; she took course 195° for Kavieng.
- **5 January.** At 0520, closing the south coast of Truk, she sighted a ship on the starboard beam at about 7,000 yards, course north at about 25 knots. She tried to close on four engines, but it was out of sight in ten minutes. Searchlights from Dublon Island opened up and swept south.

### Attack 2 — the submarine (6 January)

At 1701 on 6 January, on the way to a position southwest of Truk, *Silversides* sighted a large enemy submarine on her starboard quarter at 5,000 yards. Burlingame swung right to put the target on a 120° port track and fired three torpedoes.

- **1 min 10 s after firing:** one premature explosion about 1,000 yards off, which blanked off the view of the target.
- **1 min 50 s after firing:** a second explosion. The target's propellers, "distinct and loud until that time, stopped immediately after that."

She turned away from the firing position and went deep to avoid a possible aircraft screen, which came up again about five minutes later. Nothing was in sight but a flock of birds hovering over the target's last position, and sound heard nothing after the second explosion. There were "no counter measures as would undoubtedly been the case had the submarine survived to get off a contact report."

The target had a large naval ensign painted on the side of its conning tower. Two people were on the bridge and one lookout on top of the periscope shears, "in same position as in photograph at bottom of page 89, ONI 14." The contact table classes her as I-1 type.

COMSUBPAC's endorsement gave this only as damaged (1,995 tons), though it noted that "it is probable that the submarine and the two cargo ships were sunk." The Division 102 endorsement recommended that damage alone be credited.

### The rest of the Truk watch (7–16 January)

On 7 January at 1146 she sighted masts and commenced an approach. The target proved to be the tanker seen on 1 January with a destroyer escort astern. At 8,400 yards the tanker zigged twice away and passed out of sight. The range was too great to see whether she was loaded. On 8 January a floatplane at 0900. On 10 January, about five miles off the reef southwest of Tol, she sighted two trawler-type patrol boats and two sampans. The sampans were fishing, each with a lookout in the bow with binoculars. The patrol boats searched the area and stopped periodically to listen, and one dropped two depth charges at about 6,000 yards. That night *Silversides* ran through a fishing net and retrieved it.

On 11–12 January she saw more patrol boats and sampans. On 13–14 January she patrolled about 40 miles northwest of Truk, en route toward Namonuito. On 15 January she patrolled about ten miles south of the atoll. On 16 January at 0624 her sound picked up fast screws bearing 075° T that quickly hauled to the north for Truk, and a dark shape was seen momentarily in the very low visibility. The searchlights at Truk trained on its bearing for several minutes. At 1110 she sighted the masts of a patrol boat standing south from Otta Pass along Royalist Reef, and at 1208 a second one following in its track.

## Attack 3 — the tanker (18 January)

At 0103 on 18 January *Silversides* sighted a large tanker. She got to a position ahead on its base course of 030° T and commenced a submerged approach at 0219. An escort, an AM or small DD, was astern of the target. At 0255 she fired four torpedoes from the stern tubes. The attack unfolded in seconds:

| Time after first torpedo | Event |
|---|---|
| 46 s | First hit, in the bow, "completely obscuring bow in column of spray" |
| 61 s | Second hit, heard and felt, not seen because of the spray |
| 100 s | Third hit, under the stack aft: "Tremendous explosion and pillar of black smoke 200' high, flame and sparks at its base and out stack" |

At 0258 she went deep and began evasive tactics. The target's screws stopped immediately and were never heard again. For the next quarter hour noises through the hull sounded like the tanker breaking up.

The escort came across her stern and let go eight depth charges (Part VIII says twelve). The barrage blew the gasket off the safety vent, which they did not discover until later, and knocked out three depth gauges. It then became necessary to put a bubble in safety, and with the gasket gone the air escaped to the surface. The escort "came over with the air bubble as a marker" and dropped another barrage at 0343 that "jarred our teeth." He made another run ten minutes later, not as close, then began a supersonic search. At 0700, when she came up to periscope depth, he was about 7,000 yards to the north, angle on bow zero, and soon reversed course and pulled out of sight. A last barrage fell at 0817 when he was out of sight.

Burlingame wrote in Part VIII that had he fired bow tubes the escort "may have gotten us with that barrage as they were very close to our stern." The trim pump failure is treated under *Defects*, below. In his words, the pump defect "came very close to causing loss of this ship."

**The oil leak and the plane.** At 1102 a Zero-type floatplane sighted her and circled. At 1145 it dropped one heavy depth charge about 4,000 yards astern. That night, on surfacing, the crew found an oil leak in No. 5 fuel ballast tank that explained how the plane found them. Fuel was transferred to No. 3 FBT. A piece of the water compartment bulkhead of one of her own torpedoes was found on deck. The bridge loudspeakers were wrecked again, and the retaining lug on the main induction failed, so that shortly after she started up on four main engines, the main induction slammed shut. The slam tore the air-lock doors off in the after battery. The engines were stopped before any casualties resulted.

The contact table gives the tanker as "Genyo Maru class." COMSUBPAC credited her as a 10,010-ton AO sunk.

## 19 January — three destroyers

*Silversides* ran southeast on four engines. At 1055 she dove for half an hour on a radar indication at three miles. At 1245 she sighted masts ahead and identified three destroyers in a loose column, base course 330° for Truk. She made a submerged approach, but the minimum range was 7,000 yards on the westernmost one. They were not pinging but moved at moderate speed. She gave up at 1915 because the dive for the plane and the approach had put her too far from her main objective. Continuing would have taken her "well down into GUARDFISH's area, or possibly thru that into GREENLING's." She retired toward Truk at one-engine speed.

## Attack 4 — the convoy (20 January)

At 1250 on 20 January she sighted smoke and closed on four freighters with two escorts. The sea was glassy, which made an undetected daylight attack almost out of the question, so she took station ahead to attack submerged at dusk. After a six-hour run she had the zig plan, speed and base course. She submerged at 1818 and kept to the east of the convoy to silhouette it against the sunset.

The freighters ran in two columns of two ships; one escort led and one trailed. The leading ship in the western column looked about 7,000 tons and the others about 5,000. Burlingame later compared the largest to the *Seiwa Maru* (7,210 tons gross) and the others to the *Daifuku*, *Panama* or *Belgium Maru* (5,800, 5,270 and 5,822 tons), though they "could have been considerably larger or considerably smaller."

At about 4,000 yards the convoy zigged away, so he swung left for a straight bow shot. The geometry was unusual: the ships were so disposed that three of them presented bow to stern as a single target. He fired six torpedoes at a gyro angle of 358° and a track angle of 86° port. Hits were heard at the following times after firing:

| Time after firing | Heard |
|---|---|
| 2 min 20 s | Hit (probably the near ship) |
| 2 min 30 s | Hit |
| 2 min 40 s | Hit (probably the leading ship of the far column) |
| 2 min 50 s | Hit |
| 3 min 04 s | Hit (probably the other ship in the far column) |

The escort was dead ahead, so she went deep at once and rigged for depth charge. For the next half hour the sound of ships breaking up was clearly heard, with sharp cracks that may have been gunfire, and only one merchant screw was heard afterward where all four had been clearly audible before firing. Burlingame argued at length that the five explosions were torpedo hits: the times checked closely with estimated torpedo runs, eight-knot freighters would not drop depth charges, and the escort at about 1,000 yards would have been much louder had he dropped any.

Depth charging began at 2018. A supersonic search followed and six charges were dropped, the last at 2152, none close. Burlingame wrote, "It is fortunate that he wasn't close as we had developed an air leak which was very loud in sound gear and #6 tube outer door was jammed open." In Part VIII he adds that the escorts were "not effective." They were working under difficulties with firing range 3,000 to 4,000 yards and torpedo tracks hard to see at night. *Silversides* surfaced at 2308 and left the area at full power.

COMSUBPAC credited two cargo ships as damaged, an AK of 7,210 tons and an AK of 5,155 tons, and noted that it was probable that the two cargo ships were sunk.

## The stuck torpedo and the cascade of failures (21–22 January)

At 0056 on 21 January she sighted a patrol vessel searching to the south, ducked into a rain squall and proceeded north. She submerged before sunrise. During the day it became evident that she had a large air leak that could not be located. At sunset she surfaced and put a man over the side to look at No. 6 tube. The torpedo was stuck, half out of the tube. Packing the impeller with grease did not work, so she backed emergency and fired it. At that point the crew found a good-sized fuel leak as well.

Shortly after she started ahead, an explosion occurred in No. 1 main generator, caused by salt water that had leaked past the compensating water valve gland to No. 5 FBT and dripped on the terminals of the forward battery cable, shorting them. The gland had apparently been loosened by depth charging. At the same time the main induction operating gear broke down and revealed a box of breakfast food under the bell crank lever.

Burlingame's summary is one of the better-known lines of the patrol:

> "Thus with a dangling torpedo with war head attached, a fuel and air leak an explosion in a main generator and Cream of Wheat in the main induction, all within two hours time, it was decided to leave the area two days ahead of schedule unless leaks can be stopped."

On 22 January she submerged during the day looking for the air leaks. On surfacing she found three studs broken off the blank flange on the vent to No. 3 FBT, letting fuel escape through the vent. At 2030 a loose terminal on the main excitation switch on the auxiliary power board heated up. It could not be repaired at sea, so spare cables were made up to by-pass the switch, using the degaussing switch in the meantime. Taking stock, he found that even if the leaks could be stopped, she could not get back to Truk and still clear the area by the specified date. The fuel on hand would just permit a return on two engines, and the trip back to Truk would have meant returning to Pearl on one engine through the Marshalls and eastern Carolines. He decided to leave "in order that incoming boat may not be delayed from getting into a profitable area."

## Return to Pearl (23–31 January)

She left the area on 23 January for Pearl, on the surface except for one day submerged between Rongelap and Kwajalein Atolls. On 26 January, when 240 miles northwest ⟦sic; see note⟧ of Wotje, she sighted a two-engine bomber and submerged for 45 minutes. On 27 January, 410 miles from Wotje, radar gave a plane indication at range 16 miles; she closed to 15 and then opened out, losing it at 19 miles. She reached Pearl on 31 January.

## Damage list from depth charging and bombing

Part VIII lists the damage from the two principal events.

**Bombing, 23 December (10 items):** bow planes jammed on hard dive (electrical failure plus broken shear pin topside); all bulbs, steam tights and emergency light lenses broken in the forward compartments, conning tower and after torpedo room; cracked battery water gauge glass in the starboard forward tank; No. 1 FBT gasket blown; water in the pump room bilges that grounded the distribution panel and both high-power air compressors; nearly every voltmeter and ammeter reading incorrectly; a 240-volt battery ground on surfacing; cracked prism in No. 2 periscope; aneroid barometers knocked off the bulkheads; bridge loudspeakers wrecked.

**Depth charging, 18 January (14 items):** the air vent valve on the hydraulic line to the main induction opened, which later let the induction slam shut; a pipe plug blown out of the engine cooling water line and the stern tube flushing line; light bulbs broken aft; a ruptured head-flushing water line through No. 5 FBT leaking fuel; studs broken off the blank flange on No. 3 FBT; bridge loudspeakers ruptured again; all sea valves, stops, fuel filling and transfer valves opened from one-half to two turns; pipe plugs loosened on sea valves; collecting tank discharge valve knocked open; glands on sea valves leaking, including the one that shorted No. 1 main generator; all meters, gauges and thermometers out of calibration; holding-down nuts on the main control cubicle pilot circuit jarred loose; rivets and nuts on athwartships partitions in officers' country sheared; gasket on the safety tank vent blown.

## Anti-submarine measures

Burlingame opened Part VIII with a warning: "the Japanese are definitely improving." His observations:

- **Destroyers.** In the combat area around the Solomons they would not slow to listen, "probably due to their losses from submarine attack." On the morning of 23 December a destroyer hunted her at 22 knots without echo ranging.
- **Escort vessels in open sea.** Still used slow-speed listening combined with echo ranging, and he thought listening the more effective. On 20 January the escort's sound picked up her drain pump as soon as it was started, "at a range of at least 6000 yards since they dropped depth charges immediately."
- **Escorts trail rather than lead.** That puts them in a better position for depth charging, but it "allows the submarine freedom of movement in making the torpedo attack." He noted this on three occasions.
- **Off Truk.** Trawler-type patrol boats stopped periodically to listen, and sampans fished near them with binocular lookouts. Because of the choppy sea, "the surface and air patrol off TRUK gave us no trouble."

## Defects

Part X is the longest and most pointed section of the report:

- **Trim pump.** "I consider the defect in our trim pump an item of utmost importance." It nearly cost the ship on 18 January: when the escort's first charges blew out the safety vent gasket, the ship got heavier and he had to put a bubble in safety because the trim pump could not be used. The bubble then escaped through the vent and marked their position. "I only wish the designer of the trim pump had been with me in the conning tower then." The defect "was first reported eight months ago and no remedial action has been taken to date."
- **SJ radar.** It failed again after overhaul by the tender force. He repeated his statement from the third patrol: "It is obviously useless to install such equipment unless it can be made to function properly."
- **Pit log, gyro compass, mufflers.** The pit log failed within 24 hours of departure from a defective carbon seal ring, and later its sword arm was bent off New Ireland, probably by logs or debris. The gyro compass had a defect beyond the capacity of ship's force. All four mufflers sparked at times, "especially serious when starting up additional engines at night."
- **Fuel leaks.** Leaks in No. 5 FBT and in the vent flange on No. 3 FBT caused a loss of 4,900 gallons.
- **Depth gauges, air compressor, air leak, vent gaskets.** All depth gauges needed overhaul after depth charging, and "no two read the same." No. 2 air compressor had a broken water-cooling jacket. An unlocated air leak could be seen through the periscope. Gaskets blew off the safety, No. 1 MBT and negative inboard vents; Burlingame suggested a retaining ring on the main vent gaskets.
- **Torpedoes.** "On the first two attacks we had a premature explosion on the first torpedo fired each time. On the attack on the convoy, one torpedo stuck in the tube." Torpedo and tube routines had been meticulously carried out. Deep submergence immediately after firing, "with resultant crushing in of exploder mechanism and no immediate depth charging probably saved us from an explosion of that torpedo."

## Life aboard and remarks

**Health and habitability.** Habitability was excellent, "Coca Colas sorely missed on patrol." Health was excellent except for the appendicitis case described above. Sound conditions were excellent with "many fish noises"; no information was obtained on density layers. Radio reception was complete.

**Traffic at Truk.** Burlingame's observations on the atoll are a small intelligence study in themselves:

- It was "fairly certain" that Piannu Pass was not in use.
- Incoming high-speed traffic appeared timed to arrive from the south at the southern passes, Otta and probably Ulalu, just at daybreak.
- Searchlights on Dublon and Tol were turned on about 45 minutes before sunrise, probably to help ships through the passes.
- The immediate area south of the atoll was patrolled by trawlers day and night.
- An SJ radar "in the prevailing low visibility would have been a great help."
- The only southbound traffic was the hospital ship, which cleared Otta Pass in daylight on course 195° for New Ireland.
- The area 30–40 miles southwest of Piannu "proved profitable," possibly because traffic was routed to a point near there. One boat covering the whole southern area was "like a cat watching a half dozen rat holes at the same time."

**Recommendations.** He proposed assigning a boat to the area between 5° N and the equator south of Truk, where it should be practicable to patrol on the surface and pick up smoke in time to get into position before diving. Salat Pass was not investigated, having been saved for later in the patrol when the moon was more favorable, but "our attention was focused elsewhere."

**Personnel.** "This is the fourth consecutive patrol for six officers and twenty-one men." During the first three weeks of inactivity around Truk "the monotony was beginning to tell on peoples nerves," but the late success "was like a tonic." He closed with a line attributed to Mike Fenno: "the best music in the world is the sound of your own torpedo explosions - when they are not premature, or stuck in the tube."

**End-of-patrol figures.** Factors remaining: 9 torpedoes, 1,000 gallons of fuel, 20 days of provisions, 10 days of personnel endurance, 3,917 gallons of fresh water. Governing factor: fuel shortage "caused by use of four engines on chases after ships and convoys, plus air and fuel leaks and numerous electrical failures apparently due to depth charging," which made her leave the area a day and a half before the maximum time allowed by the operation order.

## The verdict

The three endorsements agree on the broad judgment: an aggressive patrol hampered by material failure.

**Commander Submarine Division 102 (2 February 1943).** Called the patrol "marked by the usual good judgement, aggressive action and determined attack of war-seasoned officers and crew," but "marred by material failures due in great part to depth-charge damage." Radar failure "also and again limited the effectiveness of the patrol." The division commander said verification of damage to the submarine "attacked on January twentieth" ⟦sic; the attack was 6 January⟧ was lacking and recommended crediting damage only, though one to four sinkings were quite probable. On the proposed Area South of Truk, he thought it had "some merit," but initial success would soon be countered by diversion of traffic. Submarines should be kept close in to the enemy's points of departure and destination until air reconnaissance over the open sea could be established, and the hazards of concentrated anti-submarine activity there "must be accepted." He announced a regular Navy Yard overhaul at Pearl Harbor before the next patrol.

**Commander Submarine Squadron Ten (3 February 1943).** Noted the patrol's aggressiveness "with approbation," regretted that more positive proof of sinkings could not be established, and commended Moore for the appendectomy.

**COMSUBPAC (3 February 1943; Patrol Report No. 133).** Counted 47 days on patrol and 28 on station, 15 torpedoes fired and 9 hits, for "an excellent hit percentage of 60%." Observed that better attacks came when clear of the area heavily patrolled by air and surface craft, which prevents surface running, and said this supported the experience of other boats in the same area. On the SJ radar, hoped this "will not keep the SILVERSIDES from becoming radar minded," citing a submarine that had made a night attack on a convoy in a snowstorm using radar only. "The SJ equipment must not be belittled." Closed with congratulations on "a job 'WELL DONE'."
