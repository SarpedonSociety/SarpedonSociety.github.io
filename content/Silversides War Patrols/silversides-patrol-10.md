*[[Silversides]]* went to the Marianas in the spring of 1944, a month before the American landings on Saipan. She spent 26 days in an area thick with air patrols, night-flying bombers, radar stations and heavily escorted convoys, and she fired every torpedo she carried. Four of her five attacks hit. Her best came at dawn on 10 May, when one spread of six torpedoes from the bow tubes hit five ships of a convoy that had stood out of Port Apra the day before. Her most spectacular came on the night of 29 May, when two gasoline-laden freighters blew up and burned so brightly that the escorts were blinded. Out of torpedoes on 1 June, she tracked a fresh convoy and turned it over to *Pilotfish*. COMSUBPAC called the patrol "aggressive and extremely successful" and wrote that it made *Silversides* "one of the outstanding submarines of the Submarine Force."

---

## What the histories say

The *Dictionary of American Naval Fighting Ships* gives the patrol one paragraph:

> While on her tenth war patrol, off the Marianas Islands, Silversides destroyed six enemy vessels for a total of over 14,000 tons. On 10 May, she torpedoed and sank the cargo ship Okinawa Maru; followed up with the passenger-cargo ship Mikage Maru; and then sent the converted gunboat Choan Maru No. 2 beneath the waves. Ten days later, she added to her score when she sank another converted gunboat, the 998-ton Shosei Maru. On 29 May, the submarine torpedoed and sank the cargo ships Shaken Maru and Horaizan Maru; and then headed for Pearl Harbor, arriving on 11 June. Two days later, she got underway for Mare Island Navy Yard for overhaul, returning to Pearl Harbor on 12 September.

The patrol report agrees on the shape and differs on most of the details:

- **The 10 May attack was one spread, not a sequence.** DANFS reads as three separate sinkings ("followed up with... and then sent"). The report has six torpedoes fired in 57 seconds at five overlapping ships, five hits, and two ships heard breaking up within twenty minutes. Only *Mikage Maru* was seen to sink, at 1540 that afternoon.
- **The 20 May target was a transport, not a gunboat.** *Silversides* fired at an AP she took for an *Atuta Maru*-type of about 8,000 tons, heard two timed hits, and spent the next four hours under 61 depth charges. COMSUBPAC credited the transport as damaged. DANFS credits the sinking of the 998-ton converted gunboat *Shosei Maru*, which would mean the hits went into an escort. <!-- VERIFY against JANAC -->
- **Tonnage.** DANFS gives "over 14,000 tons" for six ships. COMSUBPAC credited 23,600 tons sunk and 18,000 damaged.
- **The patrol did not end on 29 May.** DANFS goes from the gasoline ships straight to Pearl Harbor. In the report she made a fifth attack on 1 June with her last four torpedoes (all missed), then found and tracked a third convoy and turned it over to *Pilotfish*. COMSUBPAC singled this out in the endorsement.
- **The ninth patrol ended at Brisbane, not Fremantle.** DANFS says she returned to Fremantle on 8 April. The tenth patrol's prologue says she arrived at Brisbane from the ninth patrol on 8 April and was refitted there by the Submarine Repair Unit; the attack sheets give the overhaul activity as Submarine Repair Unit, Navy No. 134.

## Prologue: refit at Brisbane

*Silversides* arrived at Brisbane from her ninth patrol on 8 April 1944 for a normal two-week refit by the Submarine Repair Unit. She was docked. Four alterations were made: a Mk. III IFF set, a 40-mm gun aft on the old 3-inch foundation, VHF radio equipment, and, "most important," a new-type trim pump. She had two days of loading, was neither depermed nor wiped, and had no training period. Training was to be done on the way to Milne Bay.

## Passage to the Marianas (26 April – 6 May)

She left Brisbane at 1325 on 26 April under CTF 72 operation order 325-44. Comdr. C. C. Burlingame, her first commanding officer, rode as training officer as far as Milne Bay. The Australian corvettes *Colac* and *Echuca* escorted her and served as exercise targets, and she ran exercises for RAAF aircraft. *Echuca* dropped out on 28 April with distilling trouble.

On 1 May she moored alongside the tender *Euryale* at Milne Bay, fueled to capacity and took 15,500 gallons. The tender was "very accommodating," with many minor repairs and overnight laundry service. Burlingame left the ship there. On 2 May she made a sound test, went back alongside for another check on the bow planes, which "continue to give trouble," and got underway at 1518 up the safety lane with a new escort. <!-- VERIFY: escort name, Gemini "H.M.A.S. [?SHAN?]" --> That evening she fired all guns at a towed target. The new 40-mm "seems to be a beauty and was most accurate."

The run north passed through crowded water. She passed *Mingo* on 3 May and *Bashaw* that evening in Vitiaz Strait; her escort had a doubtful submarine contact and seemed "itchy on the trigger," so she stayed on the surface for the rest of the exercise. She released the escort north of the Admiralties at midnight on 4 May and passed *Seahorse* on 5 May. At 0600 on 6 May she shifted to CTF 17, became 17.2.24 instead of 72.1.9, and shifted her radio guard from the Australian "BELLS" schedule to NPM. She entered the southern boundary of Area 14 at 2030.

## Port Apra and Attack 1 (8 May)

At 0315 on 8 May she made SJ landfall on Guam at 33 miles. At 0515 she dove about nine miles west of Orote Point to look into Port Apra. Planes were in the air all day, usually about three at a time, "LILIES and ZEKES" working from the field on Orote Peninsula. At 0730 she counted seven ships in the harbor, several of them large transports. A shot over the reef "appears impractical."

At 1305 she picked up pinging and smoke to the north: a convoy of six or seven ships in three rough columns, standing in for the "Hole in the Wall" at Port Apra, with five escorts. Three of these were *Chidori*-type torpedo boats or large PCs. Several times the escorts had doubtful sound contacts on her and shifted to hand keying. When the convoy made a large zig to the right and left her on its port bow, the port bow escort, which already had a doubtful contact, blocked a swing for the bow tubes. She let it pass at about 1,000 yards, swung right to 060° and set up the stern tubes on a 5,000-ton MFM freighter, the last ship in the port column. The attack sheet notes it had its "masts cut down."

At 1432 she fired four Mark XVIII electric torpedoes from tubes 7–10 at ten-second intervals, range 1,300 yards, an aimed spread across the length of the target with several ships in line beyond. At 1433-17 one hit was heard. The periscope had been ducked: "torpedomen being overly ambitious in holding poppets open." She went deep when the escorts' screws speeded up. The escorts "apparently did not know where we were and were milling around aimlessly," dropping four charges, none close. At 1517 sound reported breaking-up noises, "not as loud and definite as usual," and the target's screws had stopped after the hit. At 1530 the convoy was entering Port Apra.

At 2300 she reported at least eleven ships in Port Apra: "It would be an opportune time for an air strike."

COMSUBPAC credited one 5,000-ton freighter damaged.

## Attack 2 — the convoy out of Port Apra (9–10 May)

Before dawn on 9 May, approaching Port Apra again, she saw six planes by the flames of their exhausts and dove at 0458. <!-- The time of the first sighting is illegible in the narrative (an overtyped or obliterated digit, read by Gemini as 0556). The aircraft contact table gives 0456(K). --> At 0805 a *Chidori* or PC came out, and at 0821 a seven-ship convoy followed with four or five escorts, among them a small freighter-type escort trailing. The escorts patrolled mostly to the north, which suggested a convoy bound for the Empire. One of the seven ships was a "four goal poster." The convoy zigged southwest into three columns at 3,800 yards, and she held fire and trailed submerged at high speed.

She surfaced at 1701 with Guam thirty miles astern and her battery well down, and regained the smoke at 1732. "With all the planes available at GUAM it seems a miracle this convoy had no air cover." At sunset the radar broke down and the hydraulic plant motor failed at the same moment, so she stood by hand diving stations while closing in the full moon. At 2115 the convoy's sunset zig put her in a cloud shadow and she lost it. She ran a retiring search curve at full speed and found it again at 2311, ten miles off, without radar.

The SJ came back at 0015 on 10 May (Part M records four hours of repairs, two 717A tubes and a new magnetron). At 0118 an escort challenged her at 9,000 yards; at 0137 two ships turned on red lights, and the convoy turned away. She worked around one escort at 14,000 yards, regained the convoy at 29,000 yards, ran another end-around, and dove ahead of it at 0444.

At 0530, in early twilight, she was dead ahead of a convoy in three columns. At 0539 a large left zig left her on the starboard bow, "in ideal position for an enfilading shot, as many ships were overlapping." She fired six torpedoes from the bow tubes, aiming two at the large AP leading the center column and one each at four other ships:

| 1 | ¼ length aft of MOT, four-goal-poster leading center column | 05-39-30 | Hit |
| 2 | ¼ length forward of MOT, same ship | 05-39-40 | Hit |
| 3 | MOT, leading ship, port column | 05-39-50 | Hit |

Explosions were timed at 05-41-15, 05-41-30, 05-42-15, 05-43-15 and 05-43-30. She went deep with the starboard bow escort close. One pattern passed directly over the forward torpedo room, "too close for comfort." Sound heard one ship breaking up on 110° that seemed to sink at 0554, and another on 080° that seemed to sink at 0613.

At 0735 she was back at periscope depth and saw a ship burning about three miles off with escorts around it. "Let entire crew have a look and took pictures." Two barrages of 23 and 25 charges followed at 0901 and 1010, which Coye blamed on the small engine-aft escort freighter, since the two PCs could be seen plainly and were not dropping. "Japs sounded awful mad." By 1335 the burning ship was abandoned, its back broken, bow and stern working in the swell. She matched it closely to *Mikage Maru* No. 18, 4,319 tons, in ONI 208-J. The 4-inch and 40-mm crews were called to sink it by gunfire, but a Betty appeared and "dampened our enthusiasm." At 1540, as a tube was being made ready, the ship sank bow first and a metal landing barge floated off it. Debris to the southwest (oil drums, wood, a whaleboat-type lifeboat) was heavy enough that the periscope had to be ducked to keep from striking it. She surfaced at 1845 and cruised through the debris looking for intelligence material.

COMSUBPAC credited two transports and a freighter sunk and a fourth ship damaged: a *Tatuharu Maru*-class AP (6,335 tons on the attack sheet), *Mikage Maru* (4,319), and a 5,000-ton AK, with a second 5,000-ton AK damaged. The attack sheet lists the AK as "probably sunk" on the strength of debris enough "to account for two other ships sunk." The 3rd Endorsement called this attack "particularly outstanding."

### The remnants (11 May)

At 0110 on 11 May she picked up four pips at 22,000 yards heading back toward Guam at about 6.5 knots, which suggested a damaged ship. She ran around the starboard side, skirting Galvez Bank, and dove 22,000 yards ahead at 0324 in bright moonlight. Sound conditions were unusual: the JP picked up screws at over 20,000 yards and the JK heard echo ranging at 22,000. Then the moon clouded over. By the time it was light enough to see, the convoy had gone by. "Should have delayed diving until light conditions were more favorable." Three freighters were visible, one with smoke coming from an after hold.

That night, with men in the superstructure converting No. 4 tank, two planes came by. At 2038 she dove on the second and "had an anxious moment when hatch jammed." Signalman Third Class J. S. Rindfuss "did an excellent job of getting hatch closed."

## Saipan and the PENTATHLON rotation (12–19 May)

From 12 May she rotated among sub-areas on the PENTATHLON schedule around Saipan, Anatahan and Guam. Saipan was the busiest air base she had patrolled: she sighted planes "too numerous to list individually," and the island put on a searchlight display of five lights under central control.

- **12 May.** A plane and a blinking light at dawn, then a destroyer making a high-speed pre-dawn sweep off Saipan Channel. Around noon she began an approach on smoke and pinging that turned out to be an A/S vessel with two high masts and a PC with "an extra healthy pinger." They wandered the area "making much smoke of the come hither variety." That afternoon they dropped ten charges, and five Bettys joined them to drop about 38 depth bombs three miles away. "Perhaps we were in the practice bombing area, but it seemed like a lot of live ammunition to wasted ⟦sic⟧ in practice." The contact table counts 51 charges and bombs.
- **13 May.** The same pair again. She broke off: "an unexpected reversal of course was almost embarrassing." Coye thought it was possibly the Q-ship *Sunfish* had reported.
- **14 May.** The night that taught the crew hand diving. At 0218 the SJ picked up a plane at eight miles circling to come in down moon. She dove, "or rather attempted to dive": the hydraulic pump motor would not start. On a hand dive she hung at 30 feet for several minutes because No. 2 vent, opened by hand, closed again while still showing open. "This impressed us forcibly with the necessity of practicing hand dives, which up to now we had not done." When she surfaced at 0317 the conning tower hatch would not open. A blank flange from the No. 4 conversion gear had wedged itself on top of it. At 0355 another plane came in with red and green running lights, and the JP operator "swears" he heard it pass overhead at 60 feet. "These night planes keep a patrol from becoming monotonous, but are a very potential hazard unless we get a suitable detector."
- **16 May.** Off Laulau Bay she spotted a rotating "bedspring" radar mast at 15-06-12 N, 145-45-14 E.
- **17 May.** A night radar contact developed at dawn into a lightly loaded 4,000-ton freighter with four escorts. The best shot was a bow shot at 3,500 yards in a calm sea, and she held fire. During the end-around a vessel had followed her at a constant range at about 19 knots, and once a submarine was seen astern. Three escorts put themselves between her and the target; one signaled on its sound gear; she passed within 150 yards of one with contact. "It seems possible that the only reason the escorts did not drop was because one of their own submarines was in the area."
- **18 May.** At 0232 the OOD and quartermaster sighted a possible impulse bubble at 1,500 yards with screws and high noise on the same bearing, a possible submarine contact.
- **19 May.** On the surface southeast of Guam she picked up the SJ of *Sandlance*, which was out of torpedoes, and tried without success to exchange calls. With *Sandlance* vacating the area off Port Apra, she headed there.

## Attack 3 — 61 depth charges (20 May)

She dove 16 miles west of Orote Point at 0457. Just before, three separate lights on the island had signaled; she may have been detected. She counted seven ships in the harbor and around noon began hearing random depth charging.

At 1443 she sighted smoke to the north with a Mavis and a Rufe patrolling. "During the entire approach did not change course once, but adjusted speed as necessary to keep bearing constant." The target was a transport similar to *Atuta Maru* with at least four escorts, and possibly one or more large ships well astern. At 1552 she let the target steady on a zig toward and fired four Mark XXIII torpedoes from the bow tubes, gyros near zero, track about 60° starboard, range 1,700 yards. She started deep at once with an escort close aboard to port and heard two timed hits.

The first barrage arrived as she passed 150 feet. "The four escorts and planes turned to on us with a vengeance." Close to shore, her evasion courses were limited. Every time she got three of the four astern, "they would apparently make a simultaneous run, coached on by the fourth who was pinging abeam, drop, and end up ahead of us." There was no useful temperature gradient. Sixty-one charges were dropped, including one salvo of 24. Some were close but set shallow; most were aft. At 1820 one PC was still listening at 3,000 yards. At 2000 a pinger closed her to 4,000 yards on a steady bearing and she went deep for another hour. She surfaced at 2111. The SJ had been "jarred out of adjustment" and was tuned up on land the next night; the gyro compass follow-up system also failed during this attack (Part K).

COMSUBPAC credited the transport as damaged, 8,000 tons (7,952 on the attack sheet).

## A crowded area (21–27 May)

- **21 May.** A *Mutsuki*-class destroyer patrolling at about 12 knots. For two and a half hours she made approaches with all tubes ready; his zig plan was "unpredictable and very discouraging." "If we had circled when we first picked him up he would have passed close aboard."
- **22 May.** Three submarine contacts west of Saipan in one day, all probably friendly, all diving or turning away before calls could be exchanged. "These two contacts emphasizes ⟦sic⟧ the need for some rapid means of identification and communication."
- **23 May.** An abandoned clinker-built Japanese lifeboat marked "2." That evening a saturation-size radar pip vanished at 5,000 yards, "friend or foe."
- **24 May.** A transport and an engine-aft AK hugging the shore past Point Ritidian; the closest she could get was 7,500 yards.
- **25 May.** Three night planes on the SJ off Saipan before she dove at 0345. At 0915 she found an 8,000-ton transport and a 7,000-ton four-goal-poster AK with a destroyer and AM7-class escorts and Mavis and Jake air cover. The minimum range was 3,400 yards, "excessive for calm sea." She trailed submerged, then on the surface at 15 knots on a quick charge, dove again for a plane flying from Saipan to the convoy, and chased at full speed into the night.
- **26 May.** A retiring search curve and a sweep north found only a field of oil drums. She gave up at 1951: the convoy "was probably making better speed than we had estimated."

## Attack 4 — the gasoline ships (28–29 May)

At 0554 on 28 May she dove for a Mavis and headed toward it in case it was covering a convoy. At 0726 she sighted smoke on the Empire–Saipan route: two AKs, an MFM of 5,000 tons and an MKMF of 3,000, with a *Chidori* leading, a PC on each beam and a small engine-aft escort freighter astern. The sea was calm, so she tracked from ahead all day and waited for night.

After moonset at 2340 she closed. At midnight the convoy made a large base-course change just as she started in, and she could see the ships signaling among themselves. She opened out, tracked the new course and came in on the starboard bow. At 0133 on 29 May, with the leading escort turning toward and the starboard escort dead ahead at 2,000 yards, she fired six Mark XXIII torpedoes from the bow tubes, three at each AK, with a 1° divergent spread, torpedo run 3,400 yards.

Hits came at 0135-35 and 0135-45 on the first ship and 0136-45 and 0136-51 on the second. Coye's narrative:

> Heard and saw two hits in each AK, followed by tremendous explosions and flames which lit up the whole scene almost as bright as day... Escorts were probably blinded by the holocaust which was now raging astern and added to the confusion by dropping twenty six depth charges. Both AK pips disappeared from the radar screen within three minutes from the time they were hit and it is doubtful that any of the crews survived. Ships were apparently loaded with gasoline and a million or so gallons burning is truly an awe-inspiring spectacle.

The escorts closed to 1,650 yards and opened out. She evaded on the surface. At 0400 the flames were still visible thirty miles away.

COMSUBPAC credited a 5,000-ton and a 3,000-ton freighter sunk.

## Attack 5 — the last four torpedoes (30 May – 1 June)

At 0903 on 30 May, submerged northwest of Saipan, she sighted smoke she could not close: a convoy with Mavis and Jake air cover. She surfaced at 2010, sent a contact report and ran a retiring search curve. At 0520 on 31 May she found it again; she had covered the zigs to the right first, and "he had zigged to the left. Have yet to outguess the sunset zigs." An Emily over the convoy "actually aided end-around run," since it could be seen when the smoke could not. At 1220 she dove ahead in a glassy sea and looked them over: two MFM coal-burning freighters of 5,500 tons and a KFK 3,300-ton diesel freighter similar to *Hokaku Maru*, with escorts, on the Saipan–Empire route.

At 2127 she met *Shark* and exchanged recognition signals and calls, but "communication by blinker at night is not satisfactory, and when in contact with a convoy is apt to give the show away." After moonset she dropped back for a stern-tube attack; she had no torpedoes forward. An escort turned on two red lights at 5,600 yards and speeded up but did not fire, so she went around to the port bow. At 0330 the convoy made a large left zig by column movement, and at 0333 she fired her last four torpedoes, Mark XVIIIs, two at each of the two leading ships, run 3,300 yards. She applied no spread "as inaccuracy of present TBT provides a spread at a long range." The torpedoes ran normally and all missed. The convoy never knew it had been attacked.

At 0435 she sent *Shark* a visual message wishing her better luck. At 0440 she saw a large explosion and flare-up in the direction of the convoy: "Someone was getting in an attack." At 0453 she fired a yellow, red and green rocket from a Very pistol to draw off and confuse the escorts, and set course for Pearl.

## Out of torpedoes: the third convoy (1–2 June)

At 1057 on 1 June she sighted smoke and an Emily: another convoy, course about 320°. "After we expend our torpedoes we seem to attract contacts like flypaper." She tracked it and sent a contact report to COMSUBPAC. At 1730 she sighted a submarine at 12 miles; both fired emergency identification flares and closed to visual signaling distance. It was *Pilotfish*, which "apparently had taken prompt action on our contact report." *Silversides* gave her the convoy and left the area.

On 2 June she passed between Asuncion and Agrihan on the surface, apparently undetected, sent CTF 17 a last report on the convoy, and broadcast its last position to *Flier* in case she could intercept. COMSUBPAC: "even when out of torpedoes and another convoy was contacted, by expert tracking she was able to turn this convoy over to other submarines who were successful in subsequent attacks on this convoy." <!-- VERIFY: which boats attacked the convoys of 1 June and what they sank (Shark, Pilotfish, Pintado group). -->

## Return to Pearl (3–11 June)

She ran daily dives and drills en route, diving for a Mavis and an Emily on 3 June. She rendezvoused with *PC-1081* at 0430 on 11 June and arrived at the Submarine Base, Pearl Harbor, at 0930. The division commander announced a navy yard overhaul on the West Coast.

## Anti-submarine measures

- **Escorts.** No new tactics, but the ratio of escorts to ships was rising, and they were more liberal with charges: several salvoes of 24 or more. Coye noted that each time she was near a base where they could replenish.
- **Aircraft.** Constant daylight air cover around Saipan and Guam; all convoys but one had air escort. Night-flying planes were met off both islands. "None of these attacked despite the fact several of them passed rather close."
- **Radar.** "This vessel is not equipped with an ARC or CUO." The escorts did not appear to have radar. Shore-based radar was seen and probably picked her up within 10 to 15 miles of the islands.
- **Evasion.** On the surface at night; by day, deep at 80 RPM, coming up when out of sound range.

## Defects

- **Hydraulic pump motor controller.** The drum controller was unreliable. It failed during the 9 May chase and caused the hand dive of 14 May.
- **Gyro compass.** One rotor lost vacuum; the follow-up system failed under depth charging.
- **Piping.** Recurrent failures of circulating water and fuel oil piping "incident to age."
- **SJ-a radar.** It worked through most of the patrol, but with "far more material difficulties" than the two before: 16 hours down on 30 April (grounded magnetron filaments, a burned-out resistor, a shorted capacitor), 4 hours during the 9 May chase (jitter traced to two 717A tubes, then a dead magnetron), 3 hours on 26 May, and a blank PPI on 5 June. The T-R cavity slide moved so freely that optimum tuning was "impossible to maintain while tightening the locking screws."
- **SD-a radar.** Secured in enemy waters. Friendly planes were picked up at up to 26 miles; planes at about 3,000 feet could be tracked from 26 to 16 miles, lost, and picked up again at about 9. IFF worked satisfactorily.

## Radio, sound and density layers

Reception was good on the Belconnen ("BELLS") schedules, with 12,170 kc best by day and 8,430 by night, and on the Haiku schedules (14,390 by day, 6,380 by night, with some jamming at dawn). No serials were missed. Transmitting from the area by day was "very difficult": she was jammed completely on 5,235 and 8,470 kc by at least two stations in the Marianas, and she could hear NPM but not raise him.

There were no sound gear failures. Near Guam the JK listening range was about 6,000 yards against 12,000–15,000 on the JP, and echo ranging was heard at an estimated 40,000 yards or more. Nine bathythermograph observations are recorded in Part O, from 30 April off Australia to 31 May west of Saipan. The temperature drop was usually small (½° to 6°), and on 5 May there was none.

## Life aboard and remarks

**Health, food and habitability.** No serious illness or injuries. The commissary department "did an excellent job" with careful planning and better cooking. Habitability was not as good as on earlier patrols with more surface time; a can or two of CO2 absorbent helped on long all-day dives, "but was not necessary."

**Personnel.** Coye wrote that he "has always been proud of the teamwork and fighting spirit of the officers and crew and finds it difficult to express his feelings in words. Some may call it 'morale', or 'esprit de corps', or 'the old sixty per cent' – but whatever it is, it pays off in hits." The control party had been reorganized for this patrol:

**Remarks (Part U).**
1. **Aircraft detector.** "A non-detectable, non-DF'able aircraft detector is urgently needed if night flying planes are to be avoided. SILVERSIDES was just plain lucky this patrol and avoided bombing through no effort on our part."
2. **Recognition between submarines.** With more boats per area, there were many more friendly contacts. *Silversides* had IFF and VHF but no word whether other boats used them or which VHF frequencies to guard.
3. **Mark XVIII torpedoes.** They ran normally, but their slow speed "is a big disadvantage in long range night surface attacks."
4. **40-mm gun.** There was no chance to fire it at the enemy. The mount was not designed as a wet mount but "with care and frequent greasing will function satisfactorily."

**End-of-patrol figures.** Factors remaining: 0 torpedoes, 4,780 gallons of fuel, 30 days of provisions, 30 days of personnel endurance.

## The verdict

**Commander Submarine Division Forty-Five, K. C. Hurd (14 June 1944).** Summarized each attack and approach, and judged the patrol "exceptionally well conducted in an area abundant in torpedo worthy targets and anti-submarine activity both on the surface and in the air. All convoys were heavily escorted and all but one had air cover during daylight at least." Health and morale on return were excellent. The ship would get a navy yard overhaul on the West Coast. Hurd's summary of Attack 1 gives the torpedoes as "Mk XVII" ⟦sic; Mk XVIII⟧ and the burning ship as "Mikago Maru" ⟦sic; *Mikage Maru*⟧.

**Commander Submarine Squadron Four, C. E. Aldrich (18 June 1944).** Concurred and congratulated the ship on "an excellently conducted and equally productive patrol."

**COMSUBPAC, C. A. Lockwood Jr. (19 June 1944; Patrol Report No. 454).** "Another aggressive and extremely successful one culminating a long series of similar outstanding patrols." Five "brilliant attacks," the handoff of the third convoy, and above all the dawn attack off Port Apra, in which "five ships were fired at with the forward nest of six torpedoes. The results were excellent in that five hits were made which resulted in the sinking of three ships and damaging of at least one more." Designated "successful" for the Combat Insignia Award. To date, Lockwood wrote, *Silversides* had sunk 24 ships of 133,407 tons and damaged 12 of 71,210.

### Method

The report, endorsements, contact tables and attack sheets were transcribed from page images by a language model (Gemini), using a prompt that requires uncertain readings to be flagged rather than guessed. The back matter was rerun in smaller batches after the first five-page run lost quality. Flagged readings, and readings that failed cross-checks between the narrative, the tables and the endorsements, were resolved by hand against the page images on 1 November 2026. The remainder of the SD-a radar paragraph and section (N), cropped from the page 32 image, were hand-transcribed. Cross-checks: torpedo count (24 fired, 0 remaining), hit count per attack sheet against narrative, and contact numbering against the narrative's S/C and A/C references. <!-- PENDING: full-page hand check of the remaining text, not just flagged readings. --> Errors in the original are kept as printed and marked ⟦sic⟧ once confirmed.
